## Why this matters

A blood test that searches for several cancers could extend screening to diseases without recommended screening programmes. The clinical question is whether acting on that signal improves outcomes. Finding cancer and demonstrating that screening helps are different tasks.

NHS-Galleri provides a randomised comparison from England. Its central finding deserves prominence: screening did **not reduce the combined incidence of stage III and IV cancer**, the primary endpoint. There were encouraging stage IV findings, but no mortality benefit has yet been shown. Those statements belong together when discussing the test with patients.

## How the test and the trial worked

Galleri looks for cancer-specific methylation patterns in cell-free DNA. A positive result also predicts a cancer signal origin, intended to guide diagnostic investigation. It does not itself establish a cancer diagnosis; a negative result does not exclude cancer.

The trial enrolled people aged **50–77 years**, with blood collection at up to **three annual visits**. After the baseline draw, participants were randomised **1:1**: **71,122** to blood testing and **71,128** to a control group whose samples were stored without testing, for **142,250** overall. Participants and trial staff were initially unaware of allocation.

Positive results led into NHS urgent suspected-cancer pathways. The comparison therefore evaluated a test connected to diagnostic care, rather than an isolated laboratory result. The primary endpoint covered stage III or IV disease among **12 prespecified cancer types**; stage IV within those types was a key secondary endpoint. GRAIL funded the trial, registered as **NCT05611632**.

## Primary result: no fall in stage III–IV cancer

There were **706 versus 688** primary-endpoint events in the tested and control groups. The incidence rate ratio (IRR) was **1.03**, with **95% CI 0.92–1.14; P=0.63**. The primary endpoint was not met. The interval includes both a reduction and an increase; the result does not demonstrate a reduction in combined late-stage disease.

Across successive rounds, stage III–IV IRRs were **1.19 (0.98–1.43)**, **0.95 (0.77–1.17)** and **0.88 (0.73–1.07)**. The direction changed over time, but each interval included **1**. Reading the later point estimates as proof of success would bypass the overall primary comparison. The NEJM authors call for further follow-up.

## Stage IV, earlier stages and route to diagnosis

Stage IV events totalled **342 versus 397**. After the screening rounds, the overall IRR was **0.86 (95% CI 0.74–1.00)**. By round, estimates were **0.91 (0.71–1.18)**, **0.78 (0.57–1.06)** and **0.74 (0.57–0.95)**. These findings support continued investigation, while the primary endpoint remains negative.

Stage I–II diagnoses increased: **647 versus 559**, with **RR 1.16 (1.03–1.30)**. Screen-detected cancers numbered **1,173 versus 290**, clinically detected cancers **2,464 versus 3,110**, and emergency presentations **225 versus 286**. These patterns suggest that screening changed how cancers reached diagnosis. They do not establish that the resulting treatment prolonged life.

All cancers diagnosed over **3 years** totalled **3,637 versus 3,400**. Extra diagnoses cannot automatically be counted as benefit. Their meaning depends partly on subsequent outcomes and the unresolved question of overdiagnosis. Earlier-stage detection and fewer emergency presentations are relevant findings, but neither substitutes for mortality follow-up.

## How accurate was the test?

The Nature Medicine performance analysis was **descriptive, without hypothesis testing**. Positive results occurred in **722/70,325 (1.03%)**, **518/64,498 (0.80%)** and **561/62,323 (0.90%)** across the rounds. In aggregate, **937 participants** had MCED-detected primary cancers. These performance counts should not be interchanged with the randomised trial's broader screen-detected cancer totals.

Positive predictive value (PPV), the proportion of positive results associated with cancer, was **58.0%, 50.4% and 45.8%**. A positive signal therefore required investigation, with cancer unconfirmed in a substantial share. The declining values describe the observed rounds; they do not by themselves explain why PPV changed.

Specificity was **99.50–99.60%**. Episode sensitivity was **26.7–37.2% for all cancers** and **47.6–63.4% for the prespecified types**. Cancer signal origin accuracy was **91.1–93.6%**. High specificity and useful localisation do not eliminate missed cancers. The trial's plain-language summary conveys the balance: about half of positive results were associated with cancer, while only about a third of people with cancer had a positive result.

## The FDA advisory panel (23 Sep 2026) and where approval stands

The panel considered a first-of-a-kind premarket approval application for screening adults aged **50+**, submitted by GRAIL on **29 Jan 2026**. Votes favoured safety **10–0**, effectiveness **6–4**, and benefits outweighing risks **7–2**, with **1 abstention** on the latter question.

Discussion highlighted potential value for cancers without recommended screening, variable sensitivity by cancer type and sparse cases for some types. Members questioned the word “early” in the intended use and sought clear labelling and studies after approval. The panel was clear that Galleri should not replace guideline screening for breast, colorectal, lung, cervical or prostate cancer.

An advisory vote is distinct from an approval decision. In its **4 Nov 2026** results, GRAIL said Galleri was **not FDA approved** and expected a decision by **early Q1 2027**. In the US it is currently offered through GRAIL's CLIA-certified laboratory without FDA approval. That commercial route does not resolve the questions raised by the trial.

## What it changes in practice

For patients asking about Galleri, start with the unmet primary endpoint and the absence of demonstrated mortality benefit. Explain that a positive result calls for a structured, organ-guided workup, potentially involving imaging, endoscopy and biopsy. A predicted origin helps direct that process; it does not replace diagnostic confirmation.

A negative result must not reassure someone away from assessment of symptoms or proven screening. Fewer than **1%** of participants had trial-related adverse events, with none serious. That reported safety experience is useful, but psychological outcomes and resource use remain to be reported. The burden of resolving a signal belongs in the discussion alongside the blood draw.

## India and resource-limited settings

As far as we found, Galleri is not available or approved in India. Processing in a US central laboratory raises cost and sample-logistics questions. Performance in English adults cannot establish performance in Indian populations, and PPV depends on cancer prevalence in the population being screened.

The trial operated within a high-income health system with rapid urgent-referral pathways. A positive signal can generate imaging, endoscopy and biopsy demands that strain limited capacity. Local usefulness would depend on completing those investigations and acting on the diagnosis, alongside the test's performance.

The first priorities remain proven screening approaches, including cervical, breast and oral cavity visual screening, and tobacco control. Direct-to-consumer marketing in India warrants caution while local performance, access and the consequences of follow-up remain uncertain.

## Open questions

Mortality, overdiagnosis, psychological outcomes and resource use are due in later reports. Longer follow-up must clarify whether the stage IV pattern translates into a patient benefit and how additional diagnoses should be interpreted. Differences in sensitivity across cancer types also need attention when explaining what a result can mean.

For health systems, the unresolved issue includes the work generated by positive signals. Test accuracy, diagnostic capacity and downstream outcomes need to be considered together before a screening programme can be judged useful.

## Bottom line

NHS-Galleri missed its primary stage III–IV endpoint. Its stage IV findings and changes in diagnostic routes justify follow-up, while proven screening and symptom assessment remain essential. Patients need a clear account of both the possible value of a positive signal and the limits of a negative result.
