    I
    Who gets it

    Epidemiology and risk

    The commonest solid cancer of young men, rising in incidence across high-income countries, and among the most curable cancers in adult oncology — provided the first decisions are made correctly.

    15–35 yMain peak; a later peak after ~60 y is mostly seminoma Consensus
    4–10×Risk with a first-degree relative (brother > father) Consensus
    ~2%Asynchronous contralateral primary, usually seminoma Consensus
    ~94%US 5-year outcome relative to the general population JNCCN 2025

    Incidence is highest in Northern and Central Europe and lowest in Africa and Asia; within the US it is most common in White men. The rise in incidence over recent decades is unexplained, and mortality has fallen in high-income settings while remaining higher where access to cisplatin, surgery and expert centres is limited Consensus. Older age (>40), non-White race and lower socioeconomic status are associated with higher testis-cancer mortality Consensus.

    Cryptorchidism — the dominant modifiable risk

    Undescended testis (inguinal or abdominal) raises risk in both gonads, more so on the undescended side; roughly one in ten testicular cancers arises in a man with this history. Prepubertal correction lowers later cancer risk compared with correction after puberty but does not abolish it, and its other great value is that a scrotal testis can be examined Consensus.

    Genetic & familial

    • Brothers and sons of patients: roughly 4–10× risk; familial cases present slightly younger
    • i(12p) / 12p gain in essentially all invasive adult GCTs — a lineage marker, not an inherited trait
    • Polygenic susceptibility (KITLG and others); rare moderate-penetrance variants such as CHEK2 reported

    Syndromes & gonadal development

    • Klinefelter (47,XXY): mediastinal — not usually testicular — GCT; also Leydig-cell lesions
    • Down syndrome: increased testicular GCT risk
    • Androgen insensitivity / mixed gonadal dysgenesis (Y material): gonadoblastoma → GCT
    • Peutz–Jeghers and Carney complex: Sertoli-lineage tumors (see Ch. XIV)
    • Hypospadias, testicular dysgenesis, infertility

    Exposures & associations

    • HIV: more seminoma; treat exactly as in HIV-negative men Consensus
    • Cannabis: heavy use associated with risk in cohort data — association only, no established mechanism Consensus; cannabis can also cause spurious hCG elevation Consensus
    • In-utero estrogen (e.g. DES) exposure: inconsistent evidence — controversial
    • Personal history of GCT: contralateral risk

    Testicular microlithiasis

    Defined on ultrasound as ≥5 echogenic, non-shadowing 1–3 mm foci (per image or per testis, depending on the definition). It is common and is not a screening tool: in a man without other risk factors, advise monthly self-examination and no serial imaging. Microlithiasis can accompany GCNIS, so the context matters — a history of GCT, undescended testis, an atrophic testis or subfertility moves it from incidental to relevant EAU.

    European guidance considers contralateral testicular biopsy or targeted follow-up when microlithiasis coexists with risk factors (atrophy, prior GCT, cryptorchidism, subfertility); North American practice is generally not to biopsy and to rely on self-examination.

    Self-examination and screening

    Population screening of asymptomatic men is not recommended. Education about monthly self-examination is appropriate, particularly after cryptorchidism or a prior GCT. Routine surveillance ultrasound of a normal-feeling testis is not advised.

    II
    The first decision

    Presentation, markers and the inguinal rule

    A solid intratesticular mass is cancer until proven otherwise. The operation is the diagnosis — and it must be done through the groin.

    Most men notice a painless lump, swelling or hardness; dull ache or heaviness is common, and a sizeable minority are first treated for epididymo-orchitis. Acute severe pain points elsewhere (torsion, infarction, haemorrhage into tumour). Metastatic presentations include back or flank pain from retroperitoneal nodes, cough, dyspnoea or haemoptysis from lung metastases, supraclavicular nodes, gynecomastia from hCG, hCG-driven hyperthyroidism, and superior vena cava syndrome from a mediastinal primary Consensus.

    Sequence that matters

    1. Scrotal ultrasound (both testes) — confirms an intratesticular solid lesion and checks the other side.
    2. AFP, β-hCG, LDH before orchiectomy — baseline for half-life kinetics and for histology clues.
    3. Discuss sperm banking — before orchiectomy if the other testis is absent or abnormal.
    4. Radical inguinal orchiectomy with high ligation of the cord at the deep (internal) ring; offer a prosthesis.
    5. Repeat markers after orchiectomy — the post-orchiectomy values define the S category; for stage assignment wait for expected decay.
    6. Staging imaging — CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast, chest imaging; brain MRI when indicated.

    Why the inguinal route

    The testis develops near the kidney and descends carrying its blood supply and lymphatics with it, so its lymph drains along the gonadal vessels to the retroperitoneum. Scrotal skin drains to the inguinal nodes. Opening the scrotum — by biopsy, fine-needle aspiration or trans-scrotal orchiectomy — breaches the tunica, risks local recurrence and creates an aberrant inguinal/pelvic drainage route that then has to be covered by surveillance or treatment fields. A diagnostic trans-scrotal biopsy of a suspected testicular tumor is contraindicated Consensus.

    The Chevassu manoeuvre

    When the nature of a lesion is uncertain (a small, non-palpable or marker-negative mass; a solitary testis; bilateral lesions), the testis is delivered through the inguinal incision after the cord has been isolated and clamped at the deep ring to control venous and lymphatic outflow. It is inspected on a drape; the lesion can be excised for frozen section. Benign histology allows the testis to be returned; malignancy proceeds to radical orchiectomy with high ligation — or, in selected cases, to organ-sparing surgery (Ch. V).

    If a scrotal violation has already happened, do not panic and do not hide it: document it, inform radiation-field planning (historically the ipsilateral inguinal/hemiscrotal region was added) and surveillance planning. Prior inguinal surgery, vasectomy or orchiopexy can also alter drainage Consensus.

    When chemotherapy comes first

    With life-threatening metastatic disease (respiratory failure from lung metastases, bleeding brain metastases, very high AFP or hCG in a typical pattern of spread), start cisplatin-based chemotherapy immediately on clinical and marker grounds. Orchiectomy is deferred, not abandoned: the testis is a sanctuary site and should be removed during or after chemotherapy Consensus.

    III
    Biochemistry as staging

    Tumor markers, half-lives and the S category

    Testis cancer is unusual in carrying serum markers into its TNM staging. Read them as kinetics, not snapshots.

    AFP

    Half-life ~5–7 days. Made by yolk sac (and some embryonal) elements. A meaningful, rising AFP is never compatible with pure seminoma: treat as nonseminoma regardless of the slide. Stable, mild elevations (low tens of ng/mL) need context — liver disease, hepatocellular or other GI cancers, or a constitutionally high baseline NCCN.

    β-hCG

    Half-life ~24–36 hours (sources quote up to 48 h). From syncytiotrophoblast: choriocarcinoma (very high), embryonal carcinoma, and, usually modestly, in about 15–20% of metastatic seminomas NEJM review. False positives: hypogonadism / LH cross-reactivity (testosterone suppression test), cannabis, other tumors Consensus.

    LDH

    Non-specific; reflects tumor burden, notably in seminoma. Never base treatment on LDH alone; low-level rises (≤3× ULN) should not drive decisions on their own NCCN.

    In a man labelled “seminoma”, a very high β-hCG (above ~1,000 IU/L, and particularly ≥5,000 IU/L — the S2 threshold) should prompt re-review for nonseminomatous elements; NCCN advises re-review and discussion with a high-volume centre when hCG >1,000 IU/L NCCN 2025.

    Timing rules. Draw markers before orchiectomy. The S category uses post-orchiectomy nadir values. In the absence of radiological metastases, wait for expected decay (about 4–5 weeks, ≈5 AFP half-lives) before calling stage IS. For IGCCCG risk allocation, use the values taken immediately before day 1 of chemotherapy (after orchiectomy). A slow fall or plateau implies residual disease; a transient rise early in chemotherapy (tumor lysis) is common in bulky disease and is not progression Consensus.

    IV
    What the slide says

    Pathology and immunohistochemistry

    Two therapeutic families — seminoma and nonseminoma — and one rule: any nonseminomatous element makes the whole tumor a nonseminoma.

    Seminoma

    About half of testicular GCTs. Sheets of large clear cells with fibrous septa and lymphocytes, sometimes granulomas; scattered syncytiotrophoblasts explain modest hCG. Exquisitely sensitive to radiotherapy and platinum; older median age than nonseminoma Consensus.

    Nonseminoma (NSGCT)

    Embryonal carcinoma (most undifferentiated, totipotent); yolk sac tumor (AFP; the dominant pure histology in infants); choriocarcinoma (very high hCG, haematogenous spread to lung and brain, haemorrhage); teratoma (somatic tissues; chemo- and radio-resistant, treated by surgery, can transform). Most are mixed; pathology should list every component from most to least prevalent Consensus.

    Not every germ cell tumor comes from GCNIS

    The WHO separates GCNIS-derived post-pubertal tumors (seminoma and the nonseminomas) from non-GCNIS tumors: the spermatocytic tumor (formerly “spermatocytic seminoma” — older men, indolent, rarely metastatic unless sarcomatoid, managed by orchiectomy alone; not a variant of classic seminoma) and the pre-pubertal-type yolk sac tumor and teratoma Consensus.

    i(12p) — an isochromosome of the short arm of chromosome 12 — is found in about 80% of GCTs, with the rest showing other 12p gains. FISH for i(12p)/12p gain is a tie-breaker for poorly differentiated midline tumors in young men and confirms GCT clonality in somatic-type malignancies arising from teratoma Consensus.

    V
    Precursor and preservation

    GCNIS and organ-sparing surgery

    Germ cell neoplasia in situ

    • Found in the adjacent parenchyma of about 90% of adult GCNIS-type tumors; absent in spermatocytic tumor and pre-pubertal-type tumors.
    • Thought to begin in utero; multifocality explains bilateral disease.
    • Untreated, about 50% progress to invasive GCT within 5 years Consensus.
    • Contralateral GCNIS in roughly 2–5% of men with a unilateral GCT; asynchronous contralateral cancer ~2% Consensus.
    • Options: low-dose radiotherapy (~16–20 Gy; Leydig function partly preserved, fertility lost), orchiectomy, or surveillance in selected men wanting paternity — with sperm banking first and testosterone follow-up.
    • Cisplatin chemotherapy given for the index tumor may eradicate GCNIS in some, but not reliably; re-biopsy is considered after chemo in some European practice.

    Testis-sparing (partial) orchiectomy

    Not standard for a normal contralateral testis with a marker-positive mass. Considered in experienced hands for:

    • Synchronous bilateral tumors or a tumor in a solitary testis, with normal pre-operative testosterone and a lesion small enough (commonly <~2 cm or ≤30% of volume) to leave functioning tissue.
    • Small, non-palpable, incidental lesions, often marker-negative, where a benign lesion (Leydig cell tumor, epidermoid cyst) is likely.

    Inguinal approach, cord control, frozen section of the lesion and margins, and biopsies of the remnant. GCNIS in the remnant is common and usually needs adjuvant low-dose radiotherapy or close surveillance. Counsel about fertility (bank first), testosterone replacement and the possibility of completion orchiectomy.

    VI
    Anatomy that predicts

    Lymphatic landing zones and patterns of spread

    Laterality predicts where the first nodes will be. That map underlies RPLND templates, radiotherapy fields and what the radiologist should look at first.

    Instrument 3Laterality landing-zone map

    The right gonadal vein enters the IVC and the left drains into the left renal vein. Right-sided tumors land first in the interaortocaval nodes just below the renal vessels, with paracaval/precaval spread; left-sided tumors land in the para-aortic nodes just below the left renal vein. Right-to-left crossover is more common than the reverse. Epididymal or cord invasion can involve iliac nodes; scrotal involvement or previous inguinal/scrotal surgery (hernia repair, orchiopexy, vasectomy) can open inguinal drainage. From the retroperitoneum, disease ascends through the retrocrural space and thoracic duct to the mediastinum and left supraclavicular fossa; the lung is the commonest visceral site, with liver, bone and brain much less common and prognostically worse Consensus.

    Why lumbar back pain changes the plan

    New, persistent back pain in a man with a testicular tumor usually means bulky retroperitoneal disease abutting or invading psoas, the vertebral bodies or nerve roots. That is high-volume disease: primary chemotherapy, not primary RPLND. Surgery is reserved for post-chemotherapy residual masses. Tumor-related back pain is a recognised presentation of advanced seminoma that is treated with chemotherapy first Consensus.

    VII
    Seeing the disease

    Imaging: every modality, and when PET earns its place

    Instrument 4Imaging chooser

    First-line

    Bilateral scrotal ultrasound with Doppler — solid vs cystic, intra- vs extratesticular, vascularity, the contralateral testis, microlithiasis. A solid intratesticular mass is an indication for inguinal exploration/orchiectomy.

    Selective

    Scrotal MRI only when ultrasound is equivocal (e.g. to characterise a small lesion before organ-sparing surgery, or segmental infarct vs tumor). US guidance from the ASCO GU panel discourages MRI of the testis for routine initial evaluation, while European guidance accepts it as a problem-solver EAU.

    PET: no role in characterising the primary.

    Standard

    • CT abdomen + pelvis with IV contrast — the core staging test.
    • Chest: chest radiograph; CT chest if the radiograph is abnormal, if there are abdominal metastases, or in nonseminoma/high markers (practice varies; many centres CT the chest routinely).
    • Node size: ~10 mm short axis is the conventional threshold; smaller nodes in the primary landing zone are suspicious, especially if multiple or enlarging.
    • CT false-negatives: in clinical stage I NSGCT roughly 20–30% harbour occult retroperitoneal micrometastases — the basis of surveillance relapse rates.

    Usually adds little

    • MRI abdomen/pelvis: an acceptable radiation-sparing alternative to CT, especially for surveillance; similar staging information.
    • FDG-PET/CT at initial staging: not routine. False negatives with small-volume disease; teratoma is FDG-negative. PET may help separate reactive from involved nodes in seminoma; it is not useful in NSGCT staging Consensus.
    • Borderline IIA: repeat cross-sectional imaging after 4–8 weeks before treating NCCN 2025.
    PET in residual seminoma. In the prospective SEMPET series, FDG-PET was the best predictor of viable tumor in post-chemotherapy seminoma residuals, especially masses >3 cm SEMPET 2004; a later validation showed accuracy improved markedly when PET was done ≥6 weeks after chemotherapy, with a high negative but modest positive predictive value Validation. NCCN keeps PET at ≥6 weeks for masses >3 cm NCCN 2025. The EAU 2026 update de-emphasises PET as a stand-alone decision tool because of a positive predictive value of only ~30–40% EAU 2026.

    In classic series about 45% of resected post-chemotherapy retroperitoneal masses show only necrosis/fibrosis and about 40% teratoma NEJM review; up to ~20–29% of sub-centimetre nodes may contain teratoma, though growing teratoma after observation of sub-cm residua is uncommon (~1–2%) Consensus.

    Principles

    • Most relapses in stage I NSGCT occur in the first 2 years; stage I seminoma relapses later (median ~12–14 months) and can be late Consensus.
    • Relapse is mainly retroperitoneal → abdominal imaging carries the load; chest imaging can be reduced in seminoma.
    • Minimise ionising radiation: MRI abdomen can replace CT; low-dose CT protocols where MRI is unavailable.

    TRISST (stage I seminoma)

    669 men randomised (factorial) to CT vs MRI and 7 vs 3 scans. MRI was non-inferior to CT for advanced (≥IIC) relapse (0.6% vs 2.6%), and 3 scans were non-inferior to 7 (2.8% vs 0.3% — numerically more advanced relapses with fewer scans). 5-year OS 99% TRISST 2022.

    Take-home: MRI is a validated surveillance modality in stage I seminoma; whether to cut scan frequency is a values discussion.

    Brain MRI

    Neurological symptoms; extensive lung metastases; β-hCG >5,000 IU/L; choriocarcinoma-predominant histology; poor-risk / nonpulmonary visceral disease; and at relapse Consensus.

    Bone scan

    Rarely indicated: bone pain, raised alkaline phosphatase, or extensive disease. Bone metastases are uncommon and confer poor risk in NSGCT (intermediate in seminoma).

    Mediastinal GCT

    CT/MRI chest; scrotal US to exclude a gonadal primary or burned-out scar.

    Clinical stage

    Stage I seminoma

    About 70% of seminomas are stage I; 15–20% relapse on surveillance, mostly in the retroperitoneum at a median of ~12–14 months, and virtually all are salvaged. Surveillance is preferred for anyone able to adhere NCCN. Size (≥4 cm in older series; continuous risk with size) and, inconsistently, rete testis invasion predict relapse; NCCN discourages risk-adapted adjuvant therapy, while European guidance allows risk-informed discussion. Alternatives for those who decline surveillance: carboplatin AUC 7 × 1–2 (one cycle was non-inferior to radiotherapy in a randomised trial and lowered contralateral GCT incidence) or para-aortic RT 20 Gy/10 fractions (or 25.5 Gy/17) NCCN. Avoid RT with inflammatory bowel disease or horseshoe kidney.

    Stage II seminoma

    • Borderline IIA: re-image at 4–8 weeks before acting.
    • IIA / non-bulky IIB (<3 cm): RT to para-aortic + ipsilateral iliac nodes (30 Gy for 1–2 cm; 36 Gy for 2–3 cm) or BEP×3 / EP×4; nerve-sparing RPLND at a high-volume centre (NCCN category 2B for 2–3 cm); carboplatin ×1 + involved-node RT (SAKK 01/10; category 2B) NCCN 2025.
    • IIB 3–5 cm, IIC, III: chemotherapy — BEP×3 or EP×4 (good risk); BEP×4 or VIP×4 (intermediate).
    • Primary RPLND for seminoma has long been framed as investigational; NCCN now lists it, and EAU frames it as an option in experienced centres — a live disagreement (Ch. XVII).

    Stage I nonseminoma

    Roughly 30–40% of NSGCT present in stage I; overall ~20–30% relapse on surveillance, mostly within 2 years. LVI is the dominant predictor (~40–50% relapse), with embryonal predominance and higher pT as supporting features Consensus.

    • Low risk (no LVI): surveillance preferred.
    • High risk: surveillance (accepted and preferred by many), BEP × 1, or nerve-sparing RPLND (attractive when the primary has substantial teratoma, or when chemotherapy is to be avoided).
    • Surveillance plus salvage cures ~98–99% Consensus.

    Stage IS nonseminoma

    Persistently elevated or rising AFP/hCG after orchiectomy with negative imaging is systemic disease: treat with chemotherapy as for advanced disease by IGCCCG risk, not RPLND Consensus. First exclude non-tumor causes (liver disease, hypogonadal hCG, cannabis, assay artefact) and a contralateral primary.

    Stage II nonseminoma

    • Marker-negative IIA (and selected IIB): primary nerve-sparing RPLND or chemotherapy (BEP×3/EP×4). After RPLND: pN1 → surveillance; pN2 → surveillance or 2 cycles of EP; pN3 → full chemotherapy Consensus.
    • Marker-positive stage II or most IIB: chemotherapy by IGCCCG.
    • Small equivocal nodes, normal markers: short-interval repeat imaging is acceptable; pure embryonal carcinoma can be marker-negative.

    Residual masses

    Seminoma: masses usually represent fibrosis and keep shrinking; ≤3 cm → surveillance; >3 cm → PET ≥6 weeks or surveillance; PET-positive → biopsy/resection or close imaging. Nonseminoma: resect all residual disease >1 cm with markers normalised (bilateral template; modified templates leave disease in 7–32%) — teratoma and viable tumor cannot be predicted by imaging or PET Consensus. After PC-RPLND: necrosis/teratoma → surveillance; completely resected viable GCT → consider 2 cycles of EP, TIP or VIP Consensus.

    XI
    When first-line fails

    Salvage, late relapse and teratoma's long shadow

    Some 20–30% of men with advanced disease do not achieve durable remission with first-line therapy; they remain curable, at lower odds Consensus. Confirm true progression first — rising markers with growing masses — and exclude growing teratoma and false-positive markers.

    Conventional-dose (CDCT)

    TIP × 4: in men with favourable features (gonadal primary, prior complete response), CR ~70% and durable remission in the majority Consensus. VeIP × 4 is the alternative. Salvage CDCT historically cures roughly half at first relapse after BEP Consensus.

    High-dose chemotherapy (HDCT) + ASCT

    Tandem high-dose carboplatin + etoposide: 364 men, 2-year PFS 60%, better as second line and with hCG <1,000 at HDCT start Consensus. TI-CE (two cycles paclitaxel + ifosfamide with stem-cell mobilisation, then three high-dose carboplatin/etoposide): 5-year DFS 47%, OS 52% in an unfavourable cohort Consensus. Often preferred for poor prognostic features, early (<6 months) relapse, incomplete response, or third line.

    TIGER (Alliance A031102 / EORTC 1407) randomises first salvage between TIP × 4 and TI-CE, primary endpoint OS. Accrual of 420 patients completed on 26 October 2022; patients are followed for five years, and as of November 2026 no primary results have been reported (registry lists primary completion 2031) ICR · NCT02375204. Until it reports, choice between CDCT and HDCT at first relapse is guided by prognostic scoring (the International Prognostic Factors Study Group score) and centre expertise.

    Third line and beyond

    HDCT if not yet given; otherwise clinical trials, gemcitabine/oxaliplatin-based or oral etoposide palliation, and molecular testing — NCCN added MSI/MMR and TMB testing and pembrolizumab for MSI-H/dMMR or TMB-high tumors NCCN 2025. “Desperation” surgery can cure selected chemorefractory men with resectable, marker-positive disease (often a single site, AFP-driven).

    Late relapse, growing teratoma and malignant transformation

    • Late relapse (>2 years): often AFP-positive, retroperitoneal, frequently with teratoma/yolk sac elements; relatively chemoresistant after ≥3 cycles of platinum → surgery first when resectable Consensus.
    • Growing teratoma syndrome: enlarging masses during/after chemotherapy with normal or falling markers — not chemoresistance; stop chemo and resect.
    • Somatic-type malignancy from teratoma (rhabdomyosarcoma, adenocarcinoma, embryonic-type neuroectodermal tumor, leukemia): i(12p) proves GCT clonality; treat surgically and with histology-directed therapy Consensus.
    XII
    Extragonadal

    Primary mediastinal germ cell tumors

    • Anterior mediastinum; no testicular primary (scrotal US to exclude a burned-out testicular lesion). Retroperitoneal “primaries” are often metastases from a burned-out testis tumor Consensus.
    • Klinefelter syndrome is a recognised association.
    • Nonseminoma → automatically IGCCCG poor risk, more chemorefractory (TP53 alterations implicated); seminoma can be good risk.
    • Associated haematological malignancy — acute megakaryoblastic (M7) leukemia, other myeloid neoplasms, idiopathic thrombocytopenia — clonally related (i(12p) in the leukemic cells), distinct from etoposide-related leukemia Consensus.
    • VIP × 4 is commonly chosen over BEP because thoracic surgery follows and bleomycin raises peri-operative pulmonary risk; some experts use BEP and omit late bleomycin doses Consensus.
    • Post-chemotherapy resection of residual mediastinal disease is critical — even with normalised markers, and sometimes with mildly elevated markers.
    • Anaesthesia: low FiO₂ and restrained fluids after bleomycin.
    • Salvage HDCT helps a minority (up to ~25%) with relapsed mediastinal NSGCT; refer to specialised centres Consensus.
    XIII
    The older man's testis tumor

    Primary testicular diffuse large B-cell lymphoma

    Usually localised at diagnosis, yet behaving like a systemic, sanctuary-seeking disease: CNS and contralateral-testis relapses, and a relapse curve that does not flatten.

    Who and what

    Typically sixth decade or later; the commonest testicular malignancy in older men; may be bilateral. Mostly activated B-cell-type DLBCL. Stage I–IIE in most cases, but outcomes are worse than other early-stage DLBCL Consensus.

    Why it relapses

    CNS relapse risk up to ~25% and continued relapse risk even 10 years out Consensus. The testis is an immune-privileged sanctuary behind the blood–testis barrier; the contralateral testis is a classic failure site without treatment.

    Standard therapy

    Inguinal orchiectomy (diagnostic) → R-CHOP × 6 + CNS prophylaxis (intrathecal and/or high-dose methotrexate per local protocol) + contralateral testis / scrotal RT (commonly 25–30 Gy) at the end of systemic therapy Consensus. Stage II: involved-nodal RT is often added.

    Relapse

    Chemosensitive relapse: platinum- or gemcitabine-based salvage (R-ICE, R-DHAP, R-GDP) followed by autologous transplant; early or refractory relapse is managed as for systemic DLBCL, where CAR-T therapy is used Consensus. CNS relapse needs CNS-penetrant regimens. Immune-evasion biology (e.g. 9p24.1/PD-L1 alterations) is an active research area.

    A testicular mass in a man over 60 is lymphoma until proven otherwise — still remove it through the groin, and stage with PET/CT, marrow assessment as indicated, and CSF evaluation.

    Non-germ cell

    Features suggesting malignancy

    Size >5 cm, lymphovascular invasion, necrosis, infiltrative margins, cytological atypia, increased mitoses and proliferation index, older age, aneuploidy Consensus. Malignant tumors metastasise first to retroperitoneal nodes.

    When RPLND

    Because chemotherapy and radiotherapy are largely ineffective for metastatic sex-cord stromal tumors, an early primary RPLND is considered when the primary has high-risk features, and for limited retroperitoneal disease; established metastases have a poor prognosis. Mitotane has been tried for functional metastatic tumors with mixed results Consensus.

    Other rarities: gonadoblastoma (dysgenetic gonads, Y material), mesothelioma of the tunica vaginalis, rete testis adenocarcinoma (poor prognosis), epidermoid cyst (benign; enucleation), and metastases to the testis in disseminated disease Consensus.

    XV
    Life after cure

    Fertility, hypogonadism and survivorship

    Fertility

    • Semen quality is often impaired at diagnosis, before any treatment.
    • Offer sperm banking before chemotherapy, RT or RPLND — and before orchiectomy if the contralateral testis is absent, atrophic or abnormal NCCN.
    • Non-nerve-sparing RPLND → retrograde ejaculation/anejaculation; nerve-sparing templates preserve antegrade ejaculation in most.
    • Chemotherapy can cause prolonged oligo/azoospermia; many recover over 2–5 years.

    Hypogonadism

    Check testosterone, LH and FSH at baseline and in follow-up, especially after bilateral disease, GCNIS radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Low testosterone contributes to fatigue, sexual dysfunction, metabolic syndrome and bone loss; replace when symptomatic and biochemically confirmed.

    Late effects

    • Cardiovascular: raised risk of myocardial infarction after cisplatin and/or infradiaphragmatic RT; metabolic syndrome; early arterial and venous thrombosis (avoid central lines where possible — EAU 2026) EAU.
    • Second malignancies for ≥35 years (stomach, pancreas, bladder, kidney, thyroid, sarcoma, myeloid leukemia). Etoposide-related leukemia (11q23) is dose-dependent: <0.5% at ~1,000 mg/m², <1% at 1,500–2,000 mg/m², up to ~6% above 3,000 mg/m² Consensus.
    • Cisplatin: ototoxicity/tinnitus, neuropathy, nephrotoxicity, Raynaud phenomenon. Bleomycin: pneumonitis — stop for symptoms; many stop for DLCO falls ≥25%; low FiO₂ at later surgery Consensus.
    • Anxiety, fatigue, financial and relationship effects — survivorship care is part of the cure.

    Gynecomastia

    From hCG-driven estradiol production (GCT, a minority of patients) and more commonly with Leydig/Sertoli/granulosa tumors. Can be the presenting sign.

    Paraneoplastic hyperthyroidism

    Very high hCG stimulates the TSH receptor (shared α-subunit, homologous β): suppressed TSH, high free T4 — resolves as markers fall Consensus.

    Anti-Ma2 encephalitis

    Rare limbic/diencephalic/brainstem paraneoplastic encephalitis in young men, sometimes with only a microscopic or GCNIS-level testicular lesion; search the testes when the antibody is found.

    miR-371a-3p is the most promising new serum marker — detects seminoma and nonseminoma (not teratoma), with emerging use for staging, surveillance and residual-mass assessment. The EAU 2026 update stops short of routine use because of assay standardisation, sample stability, logistics and cost EAU 2026; it is not a replacement for AFP, hCG and LDH in 2026.

    XVIII
    Beyond the clinic

    Public awareness and access

    People who chose to speak publicly

    Self-disclosed, widely documented cases that changed public awareness — listed only for their awareness impact.

    • Lance Armstrong (cyclist) — disclosed advanced testicular cancer with lung and brain metastases in 1996; treated with orchiectomy, brain surgery and chemotherapy, then returned to elite sport.
    • Scott Hamilton (figure skater) — disclosed testicular cancer in 1997 and became an advocate for cancer research.
    • Tom Green (comedian) — documented his 2000 diagnosis and orchiectomy on television to encourage self-examination.
    • Phil Kessel (ice hockey) — diagnosed in 2006 during his rookie season and returned to play within weeks.

    Access in resource-variable settings

    No India-specific outcome or availability figures could be verified for this atlas. Practical points that hold anywhere: cure depends on correct inguinal orchiectomy, reliable marker assays, cisplatin given at full dose and schedule, and access to high-volume surgeons for RPLND and to centres offering FDG-PET and high-dose chemotherapy with stem-cell rescue. Early referral of advanced, mediastinal and relapsed disease to such centres is the single most important access step. Mortality has remained higher in lower-income countries Consensus.
